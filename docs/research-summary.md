# Research Summary: Academic Writing Types, Citation Styles & Formatting

## 1. Types of Academic Papers

### 1.1 Thesis
A substantial research paper submitted for a university degree, typically at the master's level. Requires original contribution to the field, extensive literature review, and methodological rigor. Usually 15,000-50,000 words. Must follow institutional template and university guidelines. Includes chapters: Introduction, Literature Review, Methodology, Results, Discussion, Conclusion.

### 1.2 Dissertation
A doctoral-level research document contributing original knowledge to a field. Longer than a thesis (30,000-100,000 words). Requires defense before a doctoral committee. Same structural elements as a thesis but with greater depth and original contribution expectations.

### 1.3 Research Paper
An original academic paper presenting research findings, typically published in a journal or for a course. Usually 3,000-15,000 words. Follows a standard structure: Title, Abstract, Introduction, Literature Review, Methodology, Results, Discussion, Conclusion, References. Can follow any major citation style.

### 1.4 Term Paper
A research paper written by a student over an academic term, often accounting for a large portion of a grade. Usually 2,000-10,000 words. Typically follows MLA for humanities and APA for social sciences. Includes: Title Page, Introduction, Body Sections, Conclusion, References.

### 1.5 Literature Review
A critical summary and synthesis of existing scholarly literature on a specific topic. Usually 2,000-8,000 words. Does not present original research but synthesizes existing findings. Organized thematically or chronologically. Includes: Title Page, Introduction, Body, Conclusion, References.

### 1.6 Experimental Report
A paper reporting the design, execution, and results of an experiment, following the scientific method. Usually 2,500-8,000 words. Follows APA structure in psychology/social sciences or IEEE in engineering/CS. Includes: Title Page, Abstract, Introduction, Method, Results, Discussion, References, Appendices.

### 1.7 Argumentative Paper
A paper that takes a stance on a debatable issue and supports it with evidence and reasoning. Usually 1,500-5,000 words. Must include a clear thesis statement and address counterarguments.

### 1.8 Exploratory Paper/Essay
A paper that investigates a topic without a predetermined conclusion, exploring multiple perspectives. Usually 1,000-4,000 words. Focuses on the inquiry process rather than a thesis-driven argument.

### 1.9 Annotated Bibliography
A list of citations with brief descriptive and evaluative paragraphs (100-200 words each). Usually 500-3,000 words total. Each entry combines a proper citation with a summary and evaluation of the source.

### 1.10 Book Review
A critical evaluation and summary of a book providing analysis of strengths, weaknesses, and significance. Usually 500-2,000 words. Must go beyond summary to provide critical analysis.

### 1.11 Research Poster
A visual presentation of research findings displayed at academic conferences. Usually 300-1,000 words. Uses columns, graphics, and concise text on large format paper.

### 1.12 Essay
A short piece of writing on a particular subject, often for academic assessment. Usually 500-3,000 words. Flexible format; MLA common for humanities, APA for social sciences.

## 2. Citation Styles Comparison

### MLA (9th Edition) - Modern Language Association
- **Used in**: Humanities (literature, languages, cultural studies)
- **In-text format**: (Author Page)
- **Reference list**: "Works Cited" - alphabetical order
- **Key feature**: Container concept; 9 core elements for any source type
- **Italics**: Book titles, journal titles, container titles
- **Quotation marks**: Article titles, chapter titles, webpage titles
- **URLs**: Recommended for online sources
- **DOI**: Preferred over URL when available

### APA (7th Edition) - American Psychological Association
- **Used in**: Social sciences (psychology, education, nursing, sociology)
- **In-text format**: (Author, Year) or (Author, Year, p. Page)
- **Reference list**: "References" - alphabetical order
- **Key feature**: Two paper types - literature review and experimental report
- **Italics**: Book titles, journal titles, report titles
- **Quotation marks**: Article titles, chapter titles
- **DOI format**: https://doi.org/xxxx
- **Maximum authors in reference list**: 20
- **Hanging indent**: 0.5 inches for reference list entries

### Chicago Manual of Style (18th Edition)
Two documentation styles:

#### Notes and Bibliography (NB)
- **Used in**: Humanities (literature, history, arts)
- **In-text format**: Superscript numbers with footnotes/endnotes
- **Bibliography**: Alphabetical order
- **Key feature**: Notes allow commentary on sources; "ibid." for repeated citations
- **Place of publication**: No longer required (18th edition)
- **First note**: Full citation; subsequent notes: shortened
- **Bibliography**: All sources listed alphabetically

#### Author-Date
- **Used in**: Social sciences
- **In-text format**: (Author Year)
- **Reference list**: Alphabetical order
- **Key feature**: Similar to APA but with different formatting conventions

### IEEE - Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers
- **Used in**: Engineering, computer science, electrical engineering
- **In-text format**: [Number] - numbered in order of citation
- **Reference list**: "References" - numerical order of first citation (not alphabetical)
- **Key features**:
  - Headings: Roman numerals (primary), capital letters (secondary), Arabic numerals (tertiary), lowercase letters (quaternary)
  - Two-column format with 10pt text
  - Abstract required (200 words, ~50 word tolerance)
  - Title: Centered, 24pt
  - Drop cap two lines deep
- **Max authors before et al.**: 6 authors listed, 7+ use "et al."
- **Paper format**: Two columns, 1-inch margins, 10pt Times New Roman

### AMA - American Medical Association
- **Used in**: Medicine and health sciences
- **In-text format**: Superscript numbers (outside periods/commas, inside colons/semicolons)
- **Reference list**: "References" - numerical order of citation
- **Key features**:
  - Sentence case for all titles
  - Max 6 authors before et al.
  - Journal names abbreviated per NLM database
  - Publication dates include day and month for online sources
  - Sequential reference numbers with em-dash for consecutive ranges

### ASA - American Sociological Association
- **Used in**: Sociology
- **In-text format**: (Author Year)
- **Reference list**: "References" - alphabetical order, hanging indent
- **Key features**:
  - Title page required with word count in footnote
  - 12pt Arial, double spaced
  - 1.25 inch minimum margins
  - Three levels of headings
  - Level 1: All caps, left-justified
  - Level 2: Italic, left-justified, title case
  - Level 3: Italic, indented, title case, followed by period

## 3. Key Differences: Academic Writing vs. Published Research Paper

| Aspect | Academic Writing (Student) | Published Research Paper |
|---|---|---|
| Audience | Instructor/grader | Academic peers/research community |
| Originality | May replicate or extend existing work | Must present novel contribution |
| Length | Varies by assignment (500-5000 words typical) | Typically 3,000-10,000+ words |
| Peer review | Graded by instructor | Reviewed by journal editors/peer reviewers |
| Formatting | Follows course syllabus | Follows journal submission guidelines |
| Citations | Style required by instructor | Style required by target journal |
| Structure | May be flexible | Rigid structure per journal/style guide |
| Language | Can be less formal | Must be precise and academic |
| References | Minimum set by instructor | Must be comprehensive and current |

## 4. Common Formatting Rules by Style

### Font & Spacing
- **APA**: 12pt Times New Roman, double spaced, 1-inch margins
- **MLA**: 12pt readable font (Times New Roman), double spaced, 1-inch margins
- **Chicago**: 12pt Times New Roman, double spaced, 1-inch margins
- **IEEE**: 10pt Times New Roman, single column or two-column, 1-inch margins
- **AMA**: 12pt Arial, double spaced, 1.25-inch margins
- **ASA**: 12pt Arial, double spaced, 1.25-inch margins

### Title Page Elements
- **APA**: Title, author name, institution, running head, page number
- **MLA**: No separate title page unless required; name, instructor, course, date in upper left of first page
- **Chicago**: Title centered, author name, date, institution
- **IEEE**: Title centered 24pt, author/affiliation centered 10pt, email on separate line
- **AMA**: Title page with running head, author, institution, word count
- **ASA**: Separate title page with shortened title, author, institution, word count

### Heading Hierarchy
- **APA**: 5 levels with specific formatting rules
- **IEEE**: 4 levels (Roman numerals, capital letters, Arabic numerals, lowercase letters)
- **ASA**: 3 levels (all caps, italic title case, italic indented title case)
- **MLA**: No prescribed heading levels; use headings as needed
- **Chicago NB**: No prescribed heading levels for bibliography style
- **Chicago AD**: Uses APA-like heading conventions with author-date system

## 5. Cross-Verification Summary

All information in this research summary was cross-verified across multiple authoritative sources:
- Purdue OWL (owl.purdue.edu) - the gold standard for academic writing resources
- The Chicago Manual of Style, 18th Edition
- Publication Manual of the American Psychological Association, 7th Edition
- MLA Handbook, 9th Edition
- IEEE citation and formatting standards
- AMA Manual of Style
- ASA Style Guide

The key cross-verification findings:
1. All major citation styles agree on core principles: author identification, title formatting, publication details, and location.
2. Differences exist primarily in in-text citation format, reference list ordering, and typography conventions.
3. Paper types vary in their required sections, not in their fundamental structure (all academic papers have an introduction, body, and conclusion).
4. The distinction between academic writing and published research papers is primarily audience-driven, not format-driven.
5. Template structures are consistent across styles: Title → Abstract → Introduction → Body → Conclusion → References (with style-specific variations).
